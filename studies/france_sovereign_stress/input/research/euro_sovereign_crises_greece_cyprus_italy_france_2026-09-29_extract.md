Executive Summary


Executive Summary



The euro-area sovereign crises of 2010–13 were not a single event. Greece was an insolvency crisis that ended with official bailouts, a forced private bondholder haircut, and years of austerity. Cyprus was primarily a banking crisis that was resolved by bailing in bank creditors and uninsured depositors so that the sovereign would not absorb the full cost. Italy was a market-access and redenomination-risk crisis that was stabilised without an ESM programme and without a sovereign haircut, chiefly by domestic political change and by the ECB’s credible promise to buy bonds under conditionality.




Decision power is split. Markets set yields and can lock a government out of funding. National governments and parliaments must request help and pass laws. The Eurogroup and the ESM Board of Governors (the same finance ministers) decide official loans and conditionality. The European Commission and ECB staff design and monitor programmes as agents of those ministers. The ECB alone controls monetary instruments (policy rates, ELA, SMP, OMT, APP/PEPP, TPI) and can force timelines by threatening to withdraw bank liquidity, as it did in Cyprus in March 2013.




France in late 2026 sits in a different place from Greece 2010 or Cyprus 2013, but the mechanics of stress are familiar: debt above 115% of GDP and rising, a primary deficit near 3% of GDP, 10-year OAT yields near 4.5%, and an OAT–Bund spread that briefly exceeded 100 basis points. With the ECB still oriented toward containing inflation rather than easing, the available interventions are fiscal consolidation, political credibility, and, only under strict criteria, ECB anti-fragmentation tools (TPI/OMT) or, in extremis, an ESM programme. Bondholders of French OATs have not been haircut in the modern euro era; any deep crisis would first test spreads, ratings, and politics before restructuring talk becomes realistic.

Introduction



This report answers three questions. First, how did the crises in Greece, Cyprus, and Italy unfold, and what happened to public debt and bond yields. Second, who decides what when a euro-area sovereign or its banks lose market confidence. Third, what those episodes imply for France’s current fiscal and market stress.




Sources include IMF and European Commission programme documents, ECB research and official announcements (OMT, TPI), ESM materials on Greece’s private-sector involvement (PSI) and the Cyprus programme, Cyprus Public Debt Management Office annual reports, INSEE public-finance releases for France (2025–26), and contemporaneous market reporting on yields and spreads. Institutions


1. Who Decides: The Architecture of Euro-Area Crisis Management


1.1 Markets



Secondary-market yields and credit-default-swap spreads are the first signal. When the 10-year yield on a large issuer rises into a zone markets treat as unsustainable (often cited near 7% for Italy in 2011), primary auctions become expensive or fail to clear at acceptable cost. Greece lost affordable market access in April–May 2010 after spreads over German Bunds moved from roughly 300 to nearly 900 basis points. Markets also price euro exit or redenomination risk, which can widen spreads even when cash-flow insolvency is not imminent. That distinction mattered for Italy in 2011–12 and again in 2022.



1.2 National governments and parliaments



Only the member state can request official assistance, enact tax and spending measures, and pass bank-resolution laws. Parliaments can block packages: the Cypriot House rejected the 16 March 2013 levy on insured deposits. Domestic politics often become part of the crisis itself. In Italy, the November 2011 spike in BTP yields accompanied the fall of the Berlusconi government and the appointment of Mario Monti’s technocratic cabinet. In Greece, successive governments implemented twelve rounds of fiscal measures between 2010 and 2016 amid protests, and the 2015 Syriza period forced a third programme.



1.3 Eurogroup and ESM



The Eurogroup is an informal meeting of euro-area finance ministers. It builds political consensus on programme envelopes and conditions. Formally, the European Stability Mechanism’s Board of Governors (the same ministers) decides financial assistance. Key lending decisions require unanimity. The European Commission negotiates the memorandum of understanding in liaison with the ECB, and historically with the IMF, but as an agent of the ESM governors, not as a free-standing fiscal authority. Loans are paid in tranches against quarterly or semi-annual compliance reviews. Before the ESM, Greece’s first package used bilateral loans (Greek Loan Facility) plus the IMF; the EFSF then financed later programmes until the ESM became operational.



1.4 The ECB



The ECB’s mandate is price stability, not fiscal rescue. In practice its tools have been decisive:





Emergency Liquidity Assistance (ELA)
 to solvent banks against collateral, decided with Governing Council oversight. Threatening to stop ELA for Laiki Bank in March 2013 forced Cyprus to a deal within days.


Securities Markets Programme (SMP)
, 2010–12: limited secondary purchases of stressed sovereign bonds, including Italian and Spanish paper from August 2011.


Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT)
, announced September 2012 after Mario Draghi’s 26 July 2012 “whatever it takes” speech: potentially unlimited secondary purchases of shorter-maturity bonds for countries under an EFSF/ESM programme. Never activated; the announcement alone is estimated to have cut Italian and Spanish two-year yields by about 200 basis points.


APP / PEPP
: large-scale asset purchases that compressed spreads after 2015 and during the pandemic.


Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI)
, 21 July 2022: secondary purchases of 1–10 year public securities against “unwarranted, disorderly” fragmentation while the ECB normalises rates. Eligibility criteria include compliance with the EU fiscal framework, absence of untreated severe imbalances, debt sustainability, and alignment with recovery-plan and country-specific recommendations. Purchases are not capped ex ante but are designed not to loosen the overall monetary stance (sterilisation). The Eurosystem accepts pari passu treatment with other creditors.





Policy rates remain an ECB decision alone. When the Governing Council is hiking or holding rates high to fight inflation, anti-fragmentation tools can still buy bonds of a stressed country, but they do not recreate the ultra-low funding environment of 2015–21.



1.5 Banks, bondholders, and depositors



Loss allocation follows a hierarchy that evolved during the crisis. Early rescues (Ireland, early Greece) socialised bank losses onto taxpayers. By Cyprus 2013, the political preference had shifted to bail-in: equity and junior debt first, then senior bondholders and uninsured deposits if needed, while protecting deposits up to €100,000. Greece’s 2012 PSI applied that logic to sovereign bonds held by private investors. Official-sector holdings (ECB SMP bonds, later official loans) were largely carved out of the haircut, which shifted the composition of Greece’s creditors from private markets to official lenders with long maturities and below-market rates.

Greece


2. Greece: Insolvency, Bailouts, and Private Haircut


2.1 Context



After euro entry, Greece enjoyed easy market access while recording large fiscal and external imbalances. In October 2009 the new Papandreou government disclosed that the deficit was far larger than previously stated. The 2009 deficit eventually reached about 15.6% of GDP. Debt rose from roughly 111% of GDP in 2008 to 128.5% in 2009. Rating downgrades followed; by late April 2010 Greek bonds were junk-rated and market financing had effectively stopped.

2.3 What happened to bondholders



Private holders of eligible Greek-law bonds faced a coercive exchange: 53.5% nominal haircut, plus longer maturities and lower coupons on the residual claims, with a small cash/EFSF-note component. Present-value losses exceeded 60% in academic estimates (Zettelmeyer, Trebesch, Gulati). Holdouts on foreign-law bonds remained a smaller residual. ECB holdings under the SMP were exchanged on different, protected terms and were not subject to the same haircut. Official loans from euro-area partners and the IMF replaced much of the retired private debt: the debt ratio barely fell in a lasting way at first (164% in 2012, then back above 180%), but the 
quality
 of the debt improved for the sovereign because maturities lengthened (average remaining maturity rose from roughly 6 to 15 years) and average interest costs fell sharply.



2.4 Final result



Greece stayed in the euro. It paid an enormous real-economy price (unemployment, GDP collapse, political upheaval). Private bondholders took large losses once; official creditors later granted maturity extensions and softer terms rather than a second open haircut. By the mid-2020s the debt ratio had fallen from the COVID peak above 200% toward the mid-140s, helped by growth, inflation, and primary surpluses, while still remaining among the highest in the euro area.



Cyprus


3. Cyprus: Banking Crisis, Bail-In, and Capital Controls


3.1 Context



Cyprus entered the crisis with a banking system that was very large relative to GDP and heavily exposed to Greece (including losses on Greek bonds after PSI). Laiki (Cyprus Popular Bank) and Bank of Cyprus were undercapitalised. The sovereign’s debt was still moderate by Greek standards (about 71% of GDP in 2011, 86.6% in 2012) but could not absorb a full taxpayer-funded bank rescue without losing market access. Cyprus requested assistance in June 2012; negotiations dragged through elections into early 2013.

3.3 What happened to bondholders and depositors



At the banks, shareholders and bondholders were wiped or deeply subordinated. Uninsured depositors at Laiki ultimately recovered only a few cents on the euro in some assessments; at Bank of Cyprus, 47.5% of balances over €100,000 became equity. Insured deposits were protected in the final design. Sovereign bondholders were not subjected to a Greek-style PSI; the programme financed maturing public debt while the bail-in limited the rise in public debt that a full bank bailout would have caused. Capital controls lasted about two years, illustrating that euro membership does not guarantee free capital movement under national emergency measures.



3.4 Final result



Cyprus remained in the euro with a smaller, restructured banking system and a public debt ratio that stepped up by roughly 25 percentage points of GDP into the programme, then stabilised near 108–109% at exit. The political lesson for the euro area was explicit: after Cyprus, “bail-in before bailout” became the default narrative for bank failures, later codified in the Bank Recovery and Resolution Directive and the Single Resolution Mechanism.



Italy


4. Italy: Spreads, Doom Loop, and ECB Backstops


4.1 Context



Italy’s public debt was already above 100% of GDP long before 2011 (around 116% in 2009 and about 120% in 2011). Unlike Greece, Italy ran comparatively modest deficits at times and had a large domestic investor base. The 2011–12 episode was less about imminent cash insolvency than about rollover risk, bank–sovereign feedback, and fear of euro fragmentation. Italy was widely described as “too big to bail”: its stock of debt exceeded the firepower of early rescue funds.

4.3 What happened to bondholders



Italian sovereign bondholders were not haircut. They suffered mark-to-market losses when yields spiked, then recovered value as spreads compressed after OMT and later under QE. Banks, which held large portfolios of BTPs, faced higher funding costs and tightened lending; the sovereign–bank doom loop operated through balance sheets and credit conditions rather than through an open default. Households and firms paid via slower growth and tighter loan rates (IMF estimates suggested 30–40% of a sovereign-spread shock passed into firm lending rates within a quarter).



4.4 Final result



Italy never entered an ESM macroeconomic adjustment programme for the 2011–12 crisis. Stabilisation came from a combination of domestic fiscal signalling, ECB liquidity (LTROs), secondary purchases (SMP), and especially the OMT threat. Public debt remained high and rose further in later years, including during COVID, but market access was preserved. The Italian case is the template for “liquidity and credibility crisis without restructuring.”

The common sequence is: (1) adverse fiscal or banking news; (2) rising spreads; (3) feedback into banks and politics; (4) a negotiation among national government, Eurogroup/ESM, and ECB over who pays; (5) either restructuring of private claims, official loans with austerity, ECB backstops, or a mix. Bondholders fare worst when the diagnosis is insolvency (Greece) or when they are structurally junior to insured depositors inside a bank resolution (Cyprus). They fare better when the diagnosis is multiple equilibria and the ECB can flatten the risk of break-up without writing down principal (Italy).



France


6. France under Stress: What Institutions Can Still Do


6.1 Where France stands (2025–26)



According to INSEE, France’s general government deficit was 5.1% of GDP in 2025 (€152.5 billion) after 5.8% in 2024. Maastricht debt reached about €3.46 trillion, or 115.6–115.7% of GDP at end-2025, up from 112.6% in 2024. Government projections cited in September 2026 put the 2026 deficit near 5.4% of GDP and the debt ratio on a path toward roughly 119% in 2026 and 122% in 2027. Interest costs have risen from an implicit rate near 1.25% in 2020 toward about 2% in 2025, with Commission forecasts pointing higher as the stock rolls over (average maturity around eight and a half years).




Unlike the post-programme Greek years or Italy’s frequent primary-surplus tradition in earlier decades, France is not running a primary surplus. A rough decomposition of the 2025 deficit implies a primary deficit on the order of 3% of GDP (about €90 billion once interest of roughly €60 billion is set aside). That means the debt ratio rises even if interest rates merely stay elevated, unless growth outpaces the effective interest rate by enough to offset the primary gap—an arithmetic France currently fails.




Markets have begun to price a French premium. In September 2026 the 10-year OAT yield traded near 4.5–4.6%, and the OAT–Bund spread briefly exceeded 100 basis points for the first time in about fourteen years. At times French 10-year yields have even yielded more than Italian BTPs despite Italy’s higher debt ratio, reflecting political uncertainty and a less captive domestic investor base than Italy’s. Rating agencies have cut or warned (for example Scope to A+; other outlook revisions), which feeds index eligibility and bank capital treatment over time.



6.2 How a French crisis would mechanically unfold



The chain would resemble Italy more than Greece or Cyprus in the first instance:





Spread widening
 as investors demand compensation for fiscal slippage and political risk.


Higher refinancing cost
 flowing gradually into the interest bill as bonds mature.


Bank and insurer mark-to-market pressure
 on OAT portfolios, raising the doom-loop risk if confidence falters.


Political negotiation
 over a credible multi-year consolidation path (the government has floated an effort on the order of €54 billion for the 2027 budget discussion).


European surveillance
 under the revised fiscal framework and any excessive deficit procedure—relevant both for EU politics and for ECB TPI eligibility criteria.





A Cyprus-style bank bail-in is not the central French story today; French banks are not a Cyprus-sized outlier relative to GDP. A Greece-style PSI on OATs remains a last-resort taboo for a core euro issuer: it would shatter the assumption that large euro sovereigns repay in full and would transmit instantly to Belgium, Italy, Spain, and the banking system. That does not make it impossible in a true solvency spiral; it makes it a weapon of systemic consequence that governments and the ECB will try every other tool to avoid.

Relative to 2012, the toolkit is more complete (ESM, banking union, TPI). Relative to 2012, the monetary backdrop is worse for a high-debt issuer without a primary surplus: the ECB is not launching another open-ended QE wave to compress French yields as a side effect. Anti-fragmentation can still work, but only as a bridge while politics delivers adjustment—or as a shield against pure panic. It is not a substitute for a primary balance path that stabilises the debt ratio.



6.4 What bondholders should expect in a French stress scenario




Base case continuation:
 higher term premium and a persistent OAT–Bund spread; mark-to-market volatility; still full coupon and principal service.


Disorderly spread spike:
 ECB assesses whether dynamics are “unwarranted.” If fundamentals are clearly deteriorating, TPI is less automatic than many market participants assume. PEPP reinvestment flexibility was the first line in 2022; that era is ending.


Programme scenario (tail):
 France requests ESM support; OMT becomes legally cleaner; conditionality bites; bondholders are senior to any later restructuring discussion but live under MoU politics.


Restructuring scenario (extreme tail):
 only after official support and political exhaustion; would likely distinguish law of issuance, official holdings, and collective-action mechanics learned in Greece—while trying to protect the euro’s core collateral.




Conclusion


7. Conclusion



Greece shows what happens when a euro sovereign is insolvent and private creditors are still large: official loans buy time, then private claims are written down, and the economy absorbs a violent demand contraction. Cyprus shows what happens when the banks are the problem: losses are pushed onto bank investors and uninsured depositors so that the sovereign’s debt does not explode by as much. Italy shows what happens when the problem is confidence and fragmentation: the ECB can end a self-fulfilling crisis with a conditional purchase threat, provided domestic politics also turn.




Across all three, the decision map is consistent. Markets open the crisis. National governments must legislate. Euro-area finance ministers (Eurogroup/ESM) decide whether taxpayers of other countries lend, and on what conditions. The ECB decides whether to supply liquidity and whether to buy bonds under monetary-policy cover. Bondholders are protected when the official sector treats the episode as liquidity and euro integrity; they are sacrificed when the official sector treats it as insolvency or bank resolution.




France today combines Italian-style market sensitivity with Greek-style fiscal arithmetic (no primary surplus, rising debt ratio) under an ECB that is not primed to cut rates for fiscal relief. The most probable institutional response remains domestic consolidation under European surveillance, with TPI as a contingent backstop against disorderly runs—not as a permanent buyer of last resort for a widening fundamental gap. Whether that is enough depends less on a new instrument than on whether French politics can produce a credible primary-balance turn before the interest bill and the spread feed on each other.
